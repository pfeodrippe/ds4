For points outside a conductor with a charge in a cavity, the electric field depends only on the total charge enclosed. By Gauss's law, any charge inside a cavity induces an equal and opposite charge on the inner surface, and the outer surface carries the compensating charge. For external points, the conductor acts as if all its net charge were concentrated at the center.
In electrostatic equilibrium, the electric field inside a conductor is zero. This means induced charges redistribute on the surfaces to cancel any internal fields. The field outside depends only on the total charge on the outer surface, not on how charges are arranged inside cavities.
When applying the method of images to a point charge near a conducting sphere, the image charge is placed inside the sphere at a specific location that ensures the spherical surface remains an equipotential. The total field is the superposition of the real charge and the image charge.
For a neutral conducting shell with a charge inside a cavity, the inner surface acquires charge -q and the outer surface acquires +q. The field outside is identical to that of a point charge +q at the center, regardless of where inside the cavity the original charge is placed.
By the uniqueness theorem, if we can find any charge distribution that satisfies the boundary conditions (constant potential on the conductor), it is the correct distribution. This is why the method of images works.
The electrostatic shielding principle states that a closed conducting shell shields its interior from external fields and vice versa. The field outside depends only on external charges and the net charge on the shell.
When a point charge is placed inside a spherical cavity of a conductor, the induced charge on the cavity wall is not uniform. However, the induced charge on the outer surface of the conductor IS uniform because the conductor is spherical and the total induced charge must distribute to make the outer surface an equipotential.
Gauss's law in integral form: the flux through any closed surface equals the enclosed charge divided by epsilon_0. For a spherical Gaussian surface outside a conductor, only the total charge matters.
The potential inside a conductor is constant throughout, including in cavities. This means the potential difference between any two points in a cavity depends only on the charges within that cavity, not on external charges.
For a spherical conductor with an off-center cavity containing a charge, the field outside the conductor is purely radial and identical to a point charge at the center. The cavity's position and size do not affect the external field.
